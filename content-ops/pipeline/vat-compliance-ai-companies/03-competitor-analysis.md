## Per-Page Analysis

### thetaxadviser.com — "VAT challenges in AI product development" (AICPA, Jun 2026)
**Does well:** Treats the topic conceptually rather than mechanically — frames AI usage (token consumption) as a plausible base for entirely new tax mechanisms policymakers may introduce, separately from existing VAT law; flags a real and under-discussed reclassification risk (a feature that starts as a background AI enhancement can become the primary source of value in a product, changing its VAT characterization); raises real-time reporting and AI-driven tax-authority analytics as a forward enforcement risk, not just a compliance-cost one. This is genuinely forward-looking, policy-literate content, not a rehash of existing OIDAR/place-of-supply mechanics.
**Does poorly:** Stays almost entirely at the conceptual/policy level — no visible country-by-country mechanics, no India/OIDAR content, no marketplace or deemed-supplier treatment, no operational guidance a CFO could apply this quarter, and (typical of a professional-journal article) no FAQ or CTA structure to speak of.
**Why it likely ranks:** AICPA/Tax Adviser brand authority carries significant trust weight in professional tax content, combined with being one of very few sources treating "AI product development" and VAT as a single forward-looking topic rather than a generic digital-services rewrite; June 2026 recency helps in a fast-moving area.
**What would beat it:** Keep the same forward-looking framing (reclassification risk as AI becomes a product's primary value driver; enforcement-side AI/real-time reporting risk) but pair it with concrete, current-law mechanics — actual place-of-supply and registration consequences for token/API/compute billing across the jurisdictions a real AI company sells into, so the conceptual insight becomes something a reader can act on today, not just something to monitor.

### taxprofblog.aals.org — "AI Tokens and Sales Tax: Key Considerations" (Tax Notes State reprint, Jul 2026)
**Does well:** The most legally substantive treatment of the core differentiation question found in this research — named, credentialed authors (McDermott Will & Schulte attorneys) writing in a recognized trade publication (Tax Notes State); directly engages the technical heart of the "how do you tax a token" problem by walking through multiple distinct usage measures that can drive consumption (prompt size, compute resources used, output size/complexity) and treating the token as a wrapper around underlying rights/functionality rather than a product in itself. This precision is exactly the reasoning a global VAT/GST version of the topic needs and that almost nothing else in the competitive set attempts.
**Does poorly:** Confined to US sales/use tax — no VAT, no GST, no EU or India treatment at all; no marketplace/deemed-supplier angle; as a law-firm-authored trade-press piece, essentially no FAQ, no CTA, and no practitioner-friendly summary/checklist — it's written for tax professionals, not founders or CFOs directly.
**Why it likely ranks:** Named-author legal credibility + Tax Notes State's authority + academic-blog domain trust, combined with being close to alone in addressing the token-characterization question with real technical precision rather than a surface-level "AI services are taxable like SaaS" treatment.
**What would beat it:** Apply the same "look through the token to the underlying rights/functionality, and to the specific usage measure driving consumption" reasoning, but extend it to VAT/GST place-of-supply and India OIDAR classification, and translate the legal reasoning into a decision-usable framework (what does this classification actually change about registration, invoicing, or timing of liability) rather than leaving it as professional-audience analysis.

### anrok.com — "Sales tax for AI companies: What's taxable and what's not"
**Does well:** States plainly and specifically that token/API/compute billing does not fit frameworks built for flat-rate SaaS or physical goods — the clearest single-sentence articulation of the differentiation thesis found anywhere in this research; cites concrete, checkable state-level outcomes (Indiana and Illinois both concluding generative-AI chatbot access via web/API is not subject to state sales tax) rather than vague generalities; flags that bundled AI+non-AI offerings on one invoice complicate tax treatment — a real, practical edge case; closes with an honest acknowledgment that guidance is still catching up rather than overstating certainty, which is a genuine trust signal.
**Does poorly:** Entirely US state sales tax — no VAT, no GST, no EU, no India, despite otherwise being the closest keyword/thesis match to AU Corp's target topic; no marketplace or deemed-supplier discussion; no place-of-supply timing discussion (prepaid top-up vs. consumption); structurally a vendor resource page, so the natural pull of the content is toward "buy tax-automation software" rather than neutral, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction compliance guidance — it explains the problem well but stops short of resolving it for the reader independent of its own product.
**Why it likely ranks:** Anrok is an established, well-recognized brand specifically in sales-tax automation with a mature content program; this piece's title and thesis are an almost exact match to the "AI companies are distinct from SaaS companies on billing-model grounds" framing that Stage 1/2 identified as the open gap, so it captures searchers using that exact reasoning even though its jurisdictional scope is narrower than the searcher's actual need.
**What would beat it:** The same sharp "doesn't fit legacy frameworks" framing, globalized to VAT/GST (not just US sales tax) and including India, decoupled from a single vendor's product funnel, with real registration/filing next-steps rather than a segue toward automation software as the resolution.

### 1stopvat.com — "Modern AI-Agentic Commerce and Tax Compliance" / "VAT Compliance in the Era of AI-Agentic Commerce"
**Does well:** The only source found that squarely raises the liability question at the heart of the marketplace/deemed-supplier co-primary angle — when an intermediary (framed here as an AI agent) sits between supplier and buyer in a transaction, who is responsible for collecting tax: the agent, the platform, the seller, or the buyer; positions the firm as actively tracking an emerging, unsettled area rather than asserting settled rules it doesn't have.
**Does poorly:** The "AI agent" in this piece is consistently framed as a shopping/purchasing agent transacting on a consumer's behalf (agentic commerce), not an AI API/model marketplace or aggregator reselling usage/tokens to business customers — a real mismatch with what an AI API company or platform operator (AU Corp's likely reader) actually needs answered; poses the liability question but does not resolve it — stays at "this is the key issue" rather than providing a framework or current-practice answer; no India-specific content; no concrete registration or invoicing guidance.
**Why it likely ranks:** 1stopVAT has established content-marketing authority in the VAT-advisory niche generally, and is close to alone in pairing "AI-agentic" language with "VAT compliance" — very low direct competition for that specific phrase combination, regardless of how resolved the analysis is.
**What would beat it:** Ask the same liability question but aimed at the right actor — AI API/model marketplaces and aggregators, not shopping agents — and actually answer it with reference to how real platforms handle this today (e.g., primary-source evidence such as how an existing AI API marketplace currently calculates and remits VAT/GST on the invoices it issues), rather than leaving the reader with an open question.

### dodopayments.com — "Global VAT & GST Made Easy for AI-SaaS Products in 2025"
**Does well:** Genuinely useful breadth on multi-country registration exposure — cites that well over 100 countries require foreign digital-service providers to register, explicitly calls out that Indian GST rules can reach even small digital revenues earned by foreign sellers (with a dedicated sibling post specifically on Indian GST for global SaaS), and is honest that rates and thresholds change frequently enough that manual tracking is a real operational risk; sits inside a coherent content cluster (sibling posts on VAT vs. sales tax, EU VAT/SaaS, an OpenAI billing-model piece) that reinforces topical relevance and gives it real internal-linking depth across the whole VAT/SaaS/AI keyword space.
**Does poorly:** The title itself bundles "AI" and "SaaS" into one undifferentiated category — precisely the market-default framing Stage 1/2 identified as the gap AU Corp's new page needs to escape, not emulate; no token/API/compute-specific taxable-event discussion at all; no marketplace/deemed-supplier treatment; the piece's throughline is fundamentally a soft-sell toward "automate this with a Merchant of Record," so depth on the underlying compliance mechanics is traded for momentum toward the product pitch — a reader who wants to understand and manage this themselves (rather than outsource it to a payments platform) is not well served.
**Why it likely ranks:** Dodo Payments runs a large, well-linked content-marketing operation across this entire adjacent keyword space, giving this specific page topical-cluster reinforcement beyond its own individual content quality; it also genuinely does answer a real practical question (which countries require registration, roughly) that a lot of founders are searching for.
**What would beat it:** Actually separate AI usage-billing mechanics from generic flat-fee SaaS billing (the exact distinction the title collapses), and go deeper into the *why* of classification and liability rather than moving quickly to "an MoR handles this for you" — useful for a reader who wants to understand their obligations, not just outsource them.

### cggst.com — "Don't Lose Your ITC — The GST Guide for AI Companies (GST on AI Training Data in India)"
**Does well:** A genuinely AI-specific India angle rather than a generic OIDAR rewrite — walks through Input Tax Credit eligibility on AI training data, web-scraping services, and cloud infrastructure spend as inputs; names the real classification problem precisely (India's GST framework has no dedicated HSN/SAC code for "AI training data," forcing classification by analogy to database licensing, IT services, or online information supply); ties correct classification to concrete, checkable stakes — GSTR-1 reporting accuracy, audit exposure, and a specific stated penalty ceiling under the IGST Act. This specificity (real stakes, real mechanism, real classification gap) is a genuine strength almost nothing else in the set matches for India.
**Does poorly:** Scoped entirely to the *input/cost* side of an Indian AI company's own GST position (what it can reclaim on training-data and infrastructure spend) — not the *output/revenue* side (GST on tokens/API/compute billed to customers) and not the marketplace/deemed-supplier question at all; no treatment of a foreign AI company selling into India, or an Indian AI company exporting API access abroad — i.e., it addresses a genuinely different (though adjacent) slice of the problem than AU Corp's target reader (an AI company needing usage-billing and marketplace compliance) actually needs.
**Why it likely ranks:** A narrow, genuinely differentiated angle (ITC + AI-training-data classification specifically) that essentially no other source in this research covers, published by a specialized India GST advisory firm with established topical authority in GST content generally.
**What would beat it:** Bring the same rigor on classification-gap identification and concrete penalty stakes to the revenue side of the problem — GST on AI companies' token/API/compute billing to customers, and to foreign AI companies' OIDAR obligations selling into India — which is the actual content gap this topic targets and which this page does not attempt.

## Cross-Page Patterns

**What most strong pages share:**
- Clear, attributable authority signals — a recognized professional-journal brand, named credentialed authors at a law firm, or an established vendor brand in its own niche — rather than anonymous or generic advisory-blog text.
- Each stakes out exactly one narrow angle and stays there: policy-framing (thetaxadviser), legal characterization (taxprofblog), US state-tax specifics (anrok), agentic-liability question-raising (1stopvat), multi-country registration thresholds bundled with a product pitch (dodopayments), or India ITC/input-side classification (cggst). None combines usage-billing classification + marketplace/deemed-supplier liability + global-and-India scope into a single page — this independently confirms, from actual page content rather than just titles, the gap Stage 1 and Stage 2 identified from search-result patterns alone.
- A willingness to say the area is unsettled (Anrok's explicit "guidance hasn't caught up" framing, thetaxadviser's forward-looking/monitor framing, 1stopvat's open liability question) rather than asserting confident rules that don't exist yet — this reads as a credibility signal worth matching, not a weakness to avoid.
- Recency signals (2026 dates, references to live regulatory developments) appear across the stronger pages, consistent with this being an actively moving topic.

**What's uniformly weak across all of them:**
- None connects taxable-event/classification reasoning to actual multi-jurisdiction place-of-supply and registration consequences in one place — each stays inside its own jurisdictional or disciplinary lane.
- None gives a founder or CFO a decision framework ("if your billing model is X and your customer is in Y, here is what changes for you") — every page is descriptive/awareness-raising rather than decision-support.
- None clearly separates the commercial "solve it for me" mechanism (Merchant of Record) from the regulatory "deemed supplier" liability concept, despite MoR marketing content and deemed-supplier/liability discussion sitting side by side across this exact search space — a distinction Stage 1/2 flagged as actively confused in market messaging and that no page in this set actually draws.
- No page treats the timing question — VAT/GST liability arising at prepaid-credit purchase versus at actual token/API consumption — as a standalone practical compliance issue, even though it sits just beneath the surface of several pieces (thetaxadviser's real-time-reporting point, taxprofblog's usage-measure analysis).
- No page resolves the AI API marketplace/aggregator liability question with real mechanics; it is asked (1stopvat) but never answered against how an actual platform behaves.
- FAQ sections are thin-to-absent across the set as best as could be determined from the digests, and CTAs split into two weak patterns: "buy our software/platform" (Anrok, Dodo Payments) or effectively none (thetaxadviser, taxprofblog, cggst) — no page pairs solid education with an appropriately-scoped advisory next step for a reader who needs jurisdiction-specific registration and filing actually done, rather than software or a purely academic read.
- India seller-side coverage (a non-Indian AI company selling into India, or an Indian AI company exporting API/token access) is essentially absent across the set — cggst covers only the Indian company's own input-side GST; the global pieces are US-only or generalized-multi-country without India-specific OIDAR/export mechanics folded in.

## Verdict: The Bar to Clear
A genuinely more useful AU Corporate page needs to do five specific things none of these six pages do together:

1. **Carry classification through to consequence.** Match taxprofblog's precision on how tokens/credits/compute usage-measures should be characterized (rights/functionality being accessed, not a product being sold), but then actually state what that characterization changes about place-of-supply, registration thresholds, and invoicing across the US, EU, and India — not just US sales tax in isolation.
2. **Resolve the marketplace liability question, don't just pose it.** Give a real, sourced answer to who is responsible for VAT/GST on an AI API marketplace or aggregator — platform operator, underlying model provider, or reseller — grounded in how a real platform currently handles this in practice, and be explicit that India's Section 9(5) e-commerce-operator deemed-supplier mechanism is currently notified only for specific service categories and has not been extended to AI API marketplaces (per Stage 2's finding), rather than asserting or implying that it has.
3. **Draw the MoR-vs-deemed-supplier line explicitly.** No competitor page in this set separates the commercial fix (Merchant of Record) from the regulatory liability concept (deemed supplier) for the reader — this page should, clearly, since Stage 1/2 confirmed this is a live point of market confusion.
4. **Cover India from the seller/exporter side.** Nearly every India-focused source found (including the direct competitor analyzed here) addresses either the Indian buyer/importer side (RCM on inbound AI subscriptions) or the Indian AI company's own input-side ITC position — not a non-Indian AI company's OIDAR registration obligation selling into India, or an Indian AI company's obligations exporting API/token access abroad. This is open ground.
5. **Be honest about what's unsettled while still giving a usable framework.** Match the credibility pattern of the strongest pages (acknowledge genuinely unresolved areas — new AI-specific usage taxes under policy discussion, evolving state/country rulings) without letting that honesty collapse into vagueness; the gap in the market is decision-support, not more awareness-raising, since awareness-level content on "does VAT/GST apply to AI" is already well covered. None of this requires more length than the competitors use — it requires covering ground they each individually skip.
