# Setting Up a Manufacturing Plant in India: China+1 Strategy, Entity Choice, and the PLI Scheme for US Companies

*How US manufacturers evaluate China+1 diversification, structure an Indian entity, capture PLI incentives, and clear the Factories Act and FEMA requirements to actually open a plant.*

Last updated: 30 September 2026 — prepared by AU Corporate's India entry and taxation & regulatory practice.

This page is for a CFO, VP Finance, VP Operations, or general counsel at a US manufacturer weighing whether — and how — to set up a manufacturing plant in India as part of a China+1 diversification decision. It's written for someone evaluating this before an entity exists, not managing one that's already running.

The path from "we're thinking about India" to "we're running a plant" runs through several decisions that interact with each other more than most guides acknowledge:

- Whether the China+1 case actually holds for your specific product category
- Which sectors and investment levels qualify for the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, and what that scheme looks like as it transitions in 2026
- Whether a wholly owned subsidiary or a joint venture fits a manufacturing operation better than it would a services business
- Once the entity exists, what changes on both the Indian and the US side of your tax and compliance picture specifically because you're manufacturing tangible goods rather than delivering services

The Factories Act registration and environmental-clearance steps that actually let you commission a plant sit at the end of that chain, and are covered in the same depth here as the strategic and incentive questions that come before them.

A structural note before you read further: AU Corporate already has deep, dedicated coverage of the general mechanics a manufacturing entity shares with any other US-owned Indian subsidiary — FEMA/RBI filings, Section 482 and Form 5471 transfer-pricing exposure, and the subsidiary-vs-branch decision. Rather than repeat that content here, this page covers the delta that's specific to a manufacturing operation and links out to the fuller treatment on each topic. If you're looking for the complete FEMA filing calendar or the full Section 482/Form 5471 documentation requirements, the linked guides below go deeper than this page does on those specific points by design.

## Why US Manufacturers Are Looking at India Now — The China+1 Shift

"China+1" describes a specific and increasingly common decision, not a wholesale exit from China: a manufacturer keeps existing China capacity in place while adding a second production base elsewhere, so that a single-country disruption — tariffs, export controls, a supply-chain shock — doesn't take down the whole operation. India has become one of the more frequently discussed destinations for that second base, particularly in electronics assembly, industrial components, and select pharmaceutical categories, alongside Vietnam, Mexico, and other diversification destinations.

It's worth being direct about what China+1 does and doesn't mean for India specifically. India is a genuinely strong fit for some functions and categories — final assembly and sub-assembly in electronics, components manufacturing where PLI incentives apply, and production aimed at the large and growing domestic Indian market — and a weaker fit for others, particularly where China's supplier-density and component-ecosystem advantages in certain categories haven't been replicated at scale in India yet. Treating India as a like-for-like China replacement across every product category overstates the case; treating it as a serious, incentivized option for specific manufacturing functions is the more accurate read, and the one this page is built around. Government and industry sources have pointed to rising India-based production and export share in several PLI-covered electronics categories over the past several years as evidence the shift is real rather than aspirational — we've deliberately avoided citing a specific percentage or export-share figure here pending confirmation of a current, primary-sourced number (flagged in Writer's Notes for Stage 9), but the qualitative direction is well-documented across multiple independent sources.

The practical question this page exists to help answer isn't "is China+1 real" — it is — but "does it make sense for your specific product category, and if so, what does actually setting up in India involve." The rest of this page works through that in order: what PLI actually pays and to whom, how to structure the entity, what changes in your tax and compliance picture on both sides of the Pacific, and what it takes to physically open the plant.

## The PLI Scheme — Eligibility, Sectors, and the 2026 Transition

### What PLI Actually Pays — Sector Eligibility and Incentive-Rate Table

The Production Linked Incentive scheme pays eligible manufacturers a cash incentive tied to incremental sales of goods manufactured in India, measured against a base year of FY 2019-20. It isn't a single flat number: incentive rates, minimum investment thresholds, and eligibility conditions are set sector by sector, and vary meaningfully across the roughly 14 sectors the scheme has covered — electronics and mobile manufacturing, pharmaceuticals and active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), textiles, automotive and auto components, specialty steel, white goods, food processing, telecom and networking equipment, solar PV modules, drones, advanced chemistry cell (battery) manufacturing, and several others.

Because the rate and threshold structure genuinely differs by sector — and because several sectors' PLI windows are closing or have already closed as the scheme transitions (see below) — we're deliberately not restating a single incentive-rate table here with specific percentages and rupee thresholds. The figures we've seen cited across government and industry sources (incentive rates from roughly 4-6% up to sector-specific highs in the high teens to mid-20s percent of incremental sales; minimum investment thresholds ranging from a few crore for smaller categories like drones to over ₹1,000 crore for large electronics manufacturing; local value-addition requirements that scale upward over the incentive period) are directional rather than confirmed against a primary sector notification in this draft, and PLI's own transition (below) means some of these figures may already be superseded for your specific sector by the time you're evaluating this. The reliable approach is to confirm current sector-specific terms directly against the relevant ministry's PLI guidelines — MeitY for electronics, the Department of Pharmaceuticals for pharma, and so on — rather than rely on a percentage quoted secondhand, including by us. We flag the exact figures needing that confirmation in the Writer's Notes below for Stage 9's fact-check.

### Can a Foreign Company Apply for PLI Directly?

No. PLI is administered as an incentive available to companies registered and manufacturing in India — a foreign company cannot apply for or receive PLI disbursements directly. A US manufacturer captures PLI only through an Indian-registered subsidiary or joint venture that itself qualifies under the relevant sector scheme's eligibility conditions. This is a genuinely important point to get right early, since it shapes the entity decision covered in the next section: PLI eligibility attaches to the Indian entity, not to the US parent, so the choice of entity and how it's capitalized has a direct bearing on whether — and how cleanly — PLI incentives can actually be captured.

### PLI Is Transitioning — ECMS and the Closing 2026 Sector Windows

PLI isn't a static, indefinitely-open program, and treating it as one is a genuine planning risk. The scheme was introduced sector by sector starting in 2020-21, with each sector's incentive period running for a defined number of years from its own start date — meaning some sectors' original PLI windows are already closed or are closing during 2026, not opening fresh for a new entrant.

A successor framework — most commonly referenced as the Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (ECMS), sometimes discussed alongside a broader proposed Component Manufacturing Scheme — has been emerging as PLI's original electronics-components window winds down, aimed specifically at component-level (rather than final-assembly-level) manufacturing. We're not stating a specific outlay figure or finalized terms for ECMS here, because we were not able to confirm the scheme's terms against an official notification in this draft (see Writer's Notes) — some figures circulating in secondary sources should be treated as provisional until confirmed against the primary notification.

The honest planning answer for a US manufacturer evaluating this now: don't structure your India entry around the assumption that "PLI" as originally announced will be open and unchanged by the time your plant is operational. Confirm your specific sector's current window status before treating PLI eligibility as a settled part of your investment case, and build your financial model so it holds up whether the incentive lands through original PLI terms, a successor scheme, or — in sectors where windows have already closed — no incentive of this kind at all. We track this transition as part of our manufacturing-entry advisory work specifically because it changes so quickly relative to a typical planning horizon.

## Choosing Your Entity — Subsidiary or JV for a Manufacturing Operation

Most of AU Corporate's general India-entry content, including our [full subsidiary vs. branch office comparison](/india-entry-for-us-companies/us-subsidiary-vs-branch-office-india), frames the core entity decision as subsidiary versus branch office. For a manufacturing operation specifically, the more consequential fork is usually different: wholly owned subsidiary versus joint venture with a local partner.

Manufacturing ventures more often benefit from a local partner than a pure services or software subsidiary does, because land acquisition, labor relationships, and distribution access are all areas where an established Indian partner's existing relationships and local knowledge can meaningfully shorten the path to a running plant. That doesn't make a JV the automatic right answer — a wholly owned subsidiary gives you full operational control, a cleaner IP position, and avoids the governance complexity of a shared-control structure — but it's a genuine, sector-specific consideration that a services-focused entry guide wouldn't need to raise, and worth deciding deliberately rather than defaulting to whichever structure a generic guide recommends first.

On the FDI side, manufacturing sits under the Automatic Route in most categories, meaning 100% foreign ownership is permitted without prior government approval — the investment is made first and reported to the RBI afterward, the same mechanism covered in full on our [FDI Automatic Route vs. Government Approval Route](/india-business-setup/fdi-channels) guide. One narrow caveat worth flagging here and nowhere else on this page: if your China+1 move involves bringing in a Chinese equity or joint-venture partner — as opposed to simply diversifying production away from China — that investment can fall under Press Note 3's land-border-country approval requirement regardless of the manufacturing sector's own FDI status. This is not triggered by a US investor itself; it's specifically about whether a China-linked entity sits anywhere in the ownership chain. See our FDI Channels guide for the full [Press Note 3](/india-business-setup/fdi-channels) mechanism and the narrow 2026 relaxation for minority Chinese stakes.

For the general registration process, timeline, and documentation a US parent needs regardless of entity type, see our [full entity-registration process and timeline](/india-entry-for-us-companies/register-company-in-india-from-usa) — that page covers the SPICe+ filing sequence, the resident-director requirement, and apostille mechanics in depth, and we don't repeat that ground here.

## The Tax Stack — PLI Plus the Concessional Manufacturing Tax Rate

PLI and India's concessional corporate tax rate for new manufacturing companies are two separate, stackable incentive mechanisms, and treating them as one and the same understates what's actually available to a qualifying manufacturer. PLI is a disbursed subsidy, paid out based on incremental sales performance against the FY 2019-20 base year, administered by the relevant sector ministry. The concessional tax rate is a corporate tax election, unrelated to PLI eligibility, that reduces the entity's own tax liability on profits it earns regardless of whether it also receives a PLI disbursement.

As our [banking & taxation](/india-business-setup/banking-taxation) and [taxation & regulatory](/services/taxation-regulatory) guides already state, a narrower concessional rate — 15% — is available to new manufacturing companies meeting specific conditions, sitting alongside the standard 22% concessional rate most newly incorporated subsidiaries elect into and the 25%/30% standard rates that otherwise apply depending on turnover. We're intentionally not citing a specific section number for this rate here, consistent with how our existing tax pages handle it: the underlying provision sits within a still-settling Income-tax Act, 2025 framework, and we'd rather point you to the current, sourced rate table on those pages than restate an unverified citation on this one.

The combination is the point worth understanding, not either incentive in isolation: a qualifying manufacturing subsidiary can potentially capture PLI's incremental-sales-linked subsidy on top of a lower baseline corporate tax rate on its overall profits — two separate levers pulling in the same direction, evaluated and elected into separately, each with its own conditions. For the full rate table, election mechanics, and how the 15% rate interacts with the standard concessional regime, see our banking & taxation and taxation & regulatory guides; this section's job is connecting the two incentives, not re-deriving either one from scratch.

## GST on a Manufacturing Subsidiary — Technology-Transfer RCM and Capital-Goods ITC

Two GST mechanics show up more consistently in a manufacturing subsidiary's operations than in a typical services subsidiary's, and are worth understanding before they surface as a surprise in your first year of filings.

The first is reverse-charge GST (RCM) on payments a manufacturing subsidiary makes to its US parent for licensed manufacturing technology, technical know-how, or process transfer — a common structure where the Indian entity is producing under a license from the US parent's own manufacturing IP. As our taxation & regulatory guide already covers generally, payments an Indian entity makes to its foreign parent for services — which a technology license or technical-assistance fee generally qualifies as — are treated as an import of services and fall under the reverse charge mechanism: the Indian entity itself is liable to account for and pay the GST, rather than the US parent charging it, and can generally then claim input tax credit on that amount subject to the usual eligibility conditions. For a manufacturing JV or subsidiary operating under a US parent's process technology, this RCM liability on the royalty or technical-assistance stream is worth modeling into the cost structure from day one rather than discovering at the first GST return.

The second is input tax credit on capital goods — plant, machinery, and manufacturing equipment represent a scale of capital expenditure a services subsidiary rarely approaches, and the GST paid on that capital-goods spend is generally available as input tax credit against the entity's output GST liability, subject to standard eligibility and reversal conditions. Getting the capital-goods ITC mechanics right at the procurement stage — proper invoicing, timely credit claims — has a more meaningful cash-flow impact for a manufacturing entity than for most services businesses, simply because of the capital intensity involved.

## FEMA and Capital Inflow for a Capex-Heavy Manufacturing Plant

A manufacturing plant's capital inflow is typically larger and more capex-heavy than a services subsidiary's — land, buildings, and equipment financed through equity or External Commercial Borrowing rather than the working-capital-scale inflows a services entity usually manages. PLI disbursement, where applicable, adds a separate inflow and claim cycle on top of the initial capital-raising, with its own documentation trail tied to the incremental-sales performance the incentive is measured against.

We don't restate the FC-GPR, FC-TRS, and Annual FLA return mechanics here — our [FEMA compliance for US companies after incorporation](/india-entry-for-us-companies/fema-compliance-us-company-india-subsidiary) guide covers the full filing calendar, deadlines, and the RBI compounding process that applies if a deadline is missed, in more depth than a manufacturing-specific delta section needs to repeat. What's worth flagging here specifically: because manufacturing capital raises tend to happen in larger, less frequent tranches than a services entity's typical funding pattern, it's worth building your FC-GPR filing discipline around each individual capital infusion — plant financing, equipment-linked ECB drawdowns, and any PLI-linked capital adjustments — rather than assuming a single filing covers the full capital picture.

## Your US Parent's Own Tax Exposure — What's Different for a Manufacturing Subsidiary

A manufacturing subsidiary's transactions with its US parent are reviewed for transfer-pricing purposes the same way any related-party transaction is — under Section 482 on the US side, India's own transfer pricing rules on the Indian side — but the benchmarking method typically differs from the services-transaction framing our general transfer-pricing content emphasizes. Where a services subsidiary's intercompany transactions are usually benchmarked against comparable services margins, a manufacturing or contract-manufacturing arrangement — the Indian entity producing physical goods for the US parent, or under license from it — is more typically benchmarked using a cost-plus or resale-price method, reflecting the different economic substance of a tangible-goods manufacturing relationship versus a services delivery one. Getting the method selection right at the outset matters more for a manufacturing entity than it might seem, since it shapes both the Form 3CEB documentation and how defensible the pricing position is if it's ever examined.

We don't repeat the full Section 482, Form 5471/Schedule M reconciliation, Form 3CEB, or Local File/Master File/CbCR treatment here — our [transfer pricing & Section 482 guide](/india-entry-for-us-companies/transfer-pricing-us-india-subsidiary) covers all of that in real depth, and a manufacturing subsidiary is subject to the same filing and documentation framework described there. This section's job is the one genuinely different piece: the transfer-pricing method itself.

**Does a US parent's Indian manufacturing subsidiary trigger Form 5472?** This is worth addressing directly, because it's easy to conflate with Form 5471 and the two are sometimes mentioned together as if they're a matched pair for this fact pattern. They're not. Form 5471 is the correct, expected filing for a US parent's ownership of a foreign (Indian) subsidiary, and is already covered in depth on our transfer pricing guide. Form 5472, by contrast, typically applies in the reverse ownership direction: it's generally required of a US corporation that is at least 25% foreign-owned, or a foreign corporation engaged in a US trade or business, reporting its own related-party transactions to the IRS. A straightforward US parent → Indian manufacturing subsidiary structure — where the US entity is the one doing the owning, not the one being owned — does not automatically trigger a Form 5472 filing obligation for that reason alone. The exception worth naming rather than glossing over: if the US-side entity in your structure is itself foreign-owned at the 25%+ threshold, or is otherwise a reporting corporation under Form 5472's own rules, that's a separate, fact-specific question outside a straightforward US-parent-to-Indian-subsidiary analysis, and worth raising with your US tax advisor directly rather than assuming either way. We're flagging this here because getting it right — rather than either asserting an obligation that doesn't apply, or staying silent on a question a reasonable reader would have — is more useful than a generic "consult your tax advisor" line.

## Opening the Plant — Factories Act Registration and Environmental Clearances

Everything above this section is about deciding whether and how to enter. This section is about what it actually takes to commission a physical plant once the entity exists and the site is chosen.

A manufacturing unit crossing certain worker-count thresholds is required to register under the Factories Act, 1948 — commonly cited as 10 or more workers where power is used in the manufacturing process, or 20 or more workers where it isn't *(both figures need confirmation against the Act's current text and any state-level amendment before publishing — flagged in Writer's Notes)*. Registration runs through the state's Factories & Boilers or Labour Department rather than a central authority, and is a distinct process from the company incorporation and GST/FDI registrations covered elsewhere on this cluster.

Before a factory can actually commence manufacturing, it typically needs building-plan approval and a sequence of No-Objection Certificates from the Fire Department, the Water Department, and the State Pollution Control Board, along with advance notice to the chief inspector of factories before operations begin *(the specific advance-notice period — commonly cited as 15 days — needs confirmation against current state rules; flagged in Writer's Notes)*. These clearances run on genuinely different timelines depending on what's being manufactured: routine registrations and licenses of the kind covered generically elsewhere on this site typically clear in one to two weeks with complete documentation, while environmental clearance specifically can take anywhere from roughly a month to several months depending on the plant's pollution category and whether it requires a full Environmental Impact Assessment.

This is a genuine extension of, not a duplicate of, the Environmental Compliance content already on our [regulatory compliance](/india-business-setup/regulatory-compliance) guide — that page covers the Consent to Establish and Consent to Operate framework under the Water Act and Air Act, and Environmental Clearance under the EIA Notification, 2006, in general form for any physical or industrial operation. What's new here is the Factories Act layer specifically: the worker-count registration trigger, the NOC sequence, and the pre-commencement notice requirement that sit alongside, not instead of, the environmental-clearance process already covered there.

## Where to Locate — State Selection for PLI-Relevant Factors

State selection matters more for a manufacturing operation than for most services entities, because land availability, labor-market depth, logistics infrastructure, and existing sector clusters all vary meaningfully by state — and several of those factors interact directly with PLI eligibility and disbursement, not just general ease of doing business. A PLI-eligible electronics manufacturer benefits from proximity to an existing component-supplier ecosystem in a way a standalone software delivery center never has to consider; a plant's logistics costs and port access matter in a way an office lease doesn't.

As a starting, comparative framework rather than a ranking: SEZ status in a given state or industrial corridor can affect duty treatment on inputs and exports: existing sector-cluster presence (electronics manufacturing clusters, automotive-component belts, pharmaceutical manufacturing hubs) shortens the supplier-sourcing runway meaningfully versus building in a location with no existing ecosystem; and logistics — proximity to ports for export-oriented manufacturing, or to end markets for domestic-demand-oriented production — is a real cost driver over the life of the plant, not a one-time setup consideration. Our own state-selection content elsewhere on the site notes that manufacturing investors have tended to favor states like Gujarat and Tamil Nadu on sector-ecosystem grounds specifically — see our [guide to choosing a state to register in](/blog/best-state-to-register-company-in-india) for the fuller state-by-state comparison, including the stamp-duty and compliance factors that apply regardless of sector.

We're deliberately keeping this section to comparative fact rather than a site-selection consulting pitch — state choice is a genuine, evidence-based decision factor for a manufacturing entry, not an upsell opportunity, and the right level of depth here is helping you ask the right questions, not selling you an answer.

## FAQ

**1. Is India a good manufacturing destination for US companies moving from China?**
For specific functions, yes — it's a genuine, incentivized option, not a universal one. India has become a serious second-base destination in electronics assembly and components, industrial components, and select pharmaceutical categories, supported by PLI incentives and a large domestic market. It's a weaker fit where China's supplier-density and component-ecosystem advantages haven't been matched in India yet at comparable scale. The realistic framing is "China+1" — adding India capacity alongside existing China operations — rather than a wholesale replacement, and the right answer depends heavily on your specific product category rather than a general industry trend.

**2. What is PLI scheme eligibility for foreign manufacturers?**
A foreign manufacturer doesn't apply for PLI directly — eligibility and disbursement attach to an Indian-registered entity, which a foreign company reaches by incorporating a subsidiary or forming a joint venture in India. From there, eligibility is sector-specific: PLI covers roughly 14 sectors (electronics, pharmaceuticals, textiles, automotive components, and others), each with its own minimum investment threshold, incentive rate, and local value-addition requirement measured against incremental sales over the FY 2019-20 base year. Because terms vary meaningfully by sector — and several sector windows are transitioning during 2026 — the reliable next step is confirming your specific sector's current terms directly against the relevant ministry's guidelines rather than relying on a general eligibility summary.

**3. How much investment is required for the PLI scheme as a foreign company or subsidiary?**
There's no single figure — minimum investment thresholds are set sector by sector and have historically ranged from a few crore rupees for smaller categories to over a thousand crore for large-scale electronics manufacturing. Rather than repeat a specific number here that may not apply to your sector or may already be superseded by the 2026 transition, the more useful step is confirming the current threshold for your specific sector directly against the relevant ministry's PLI notification.

**4. Can a foreign company apply for the PLI scheme directly?**
No. PLI is available only to companies registered and manufacturing in India. A foreign company captures PLI exclusively through an Indian-registered subsidiary or joint venture that itself meets the relevant sector's eligibility conditions — there's no direct-application path for an entity incorporated outside India.

**5. Should a company plan around PLI, or its successor scheme (ECMS)?**
Plan around the honest current picture, not the original PLI announcement. Original PLI sector windows are closing on a rolling basis through 2026, and a successor framework — most often referenced as the Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (ECMS), sometimes discussed alongside a broader proposed Component Manufacturing Scheme — is emerging specifically for component-level manufacturing as the electronics PLI window winds down. We haven't confirmed ECMS's finalized terms against an official notification as of this writing, so treat any specific outlay figure or eligibility detail you see cited elsewhere as provisional until you've checked it against the primary source. The practical takeaway: confirm your specific sector's current window status before building an investment case around PLI eligibility as a settled fact.

**6. Does a US parent's Indian manufacturing subsidiary trigger Form 5472?**
Generally, no — and this is worth stating plainly because Form 5471 and Form 5472 are sometimes mentioned together as if they apply the same way here. Form 5471 is the correct filing for a US parent's ownership of a foreign (Indian) subsidiary. Form 5472 typically runs the other direction: it applies to a US corporation that is at least 25% foreign-owned, or a foreign corporation engaged in a US trade or business, reporting its own related-party transactions. A standard US-parent-owns-Indian-subsidiary structure doesn't trigger Form 5472 on that basis alone. The exception: if the US-side entity in your structure is itself foreign-owned at the relevant threshold, that's a separate, fact-specific question worth raising directly with your US tax advisor rather than assuming.

**7. Is Press Note 3 relevant to a US manufacturer's India entry?**
Only if your China+1 move involves a Chinese equity or joint-venture partner somewhere in the ownership chain — not because a US investor itself triggers it. Press Note 3 requires prior government approval for investment originating from, or beneficially owned by an entity in, a country sharing a land border with India, China included. A straightforward US-parent manufacturing entry, with no Chinese ownership link, doesn't fall under Press Note 3's approval requirement. See our [FDI Automatic Route vs. Government Approval Route](/india-business-setup/fdi-channels) guide for the full mechanism and the narrow 2026 relaxation for minority Chinese stakes.
