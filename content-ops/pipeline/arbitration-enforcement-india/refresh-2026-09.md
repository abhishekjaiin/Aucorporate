## New Authoritative Information Found
Verified via WebSearch across primary/authoritative and legal-industry sources; every legal claim added is grounded in a verifiable statutory provision or a real, citable judgment (no fabricated case names or holdings):
- **Section 34(3)**: a domestic award may be challenged within 3 months of receipt, extendable by a further 30 days only on sufficient cause, with no extension possible beyond that combined window.
- **Section 36 / decree execution**: once the Section 34 window lapses or a challenge is dismissed, the award is enforceable as a court decree; execution itself is subject to a 12-year limitation period under **Article 136 of the Limitation Act, 1963** (the general period for executing any civil court decree).
- **2015 amendment**: filing a Section 34 challenge does not automatically stay enforcement under Section 36 — a separate stay application is required, typically conditioned on a deposit.
- **2021 amendment, second proviso to Section 36(3)**: allows an *unconditional* stay where a prima facie case of fraud or corruption in the arbitration agreement or the making of the award is shown — the one narrow exception to the "deposit-conditioned stay" default.
- **Section 34(2) / Explanation 1 (2015 amendment)**: narrows "public policy" as a ground to set aside a domestic award to fraud/corruption, contravention of the fundamental policy of Indian law, or conflict with the most basic notions of morality or justice — explicitly not a merits review.
- **Section 34(2A) — "patent illegality"**: a distinct ground, added by the 2015 amendment, available only for domestic arbitrations that are *not* international commercial arbitrations, and never through re-appreciation of evidence.
- **Gayatri Balasamy v. ISG Novasoft Technologies Ltd. (2025 INSC 605)** — verified via multiple independent sources (Global Arbitration Review, IBA, Kluwer Arbitration Blog, Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas Dispute Resolution Blog, IJLLR, Singhania & Co.): a five-judge Constitution Bench, led by CJI Sanjiv Khanna, held 4:1 on 30 April 2025 that Section 34 courts have a *limited* power to modify (not just set aside) an award — severance, correcting clerical/computational/typographical errors, modifying post-award interest, and, sparingly, invoking Article 142 for "complete justice."
- **Part II, Sections 44-52 and Section 47**: procedural requirements for a foreign-award enforcement application (original award/authenticated copy, original arbitration agreement, proof the award meets the Section 44 definition), and that there is no Section 34-equivalent "set aside" proceeding available in India for a foreign award.
- **Section 48**: exhaustive, narrowly-construed refusal grounds; the 2015 amendment's narrowing of "public policy" applies equally to Section 48; the Section 34(2A) "patent illegality" ground is confirmed as *not* extending to foreign-award enforcement.
- **Government of India v. Vedanta Limited (2020) 10 SCC 1** — verified via CaseMine, SCC Online, Mondaq, Kluwer Arbitration Blog, and the underlying Indian Kanoon judgment text: resolved a genuine High Court split (Bombay HC applying Article 137 vs. Madras/Delhi HCs applying Article 136) by holding foreign-award enforcement applications are governed by **Article 137 of the Limitation Act (3 years)**, not Article 136 (12 years).
- **Nagaraj V. Mylandla v. PI Opportunities Fund-I (2026 INSC 298)** — verified via SCC Online, Mondaq (two separate write-ups), Daily Jus/Jus Mundi, and The Leaflet: decided 25 March 2026, recognising for the first time the doctrine of **transnational issue estoppel** in Section 48 proceedings — an enforcement court cannot be used to re-litigate a factual/contractual issue already decided between the same parties by a competent court at the seat.

Both Supreme Court cases are new since the page was last written (the page previously cited only the 2026 Patna High Court ruling) and directly address the "recent Supreme Court guidance" gap flagged in the task brief.
